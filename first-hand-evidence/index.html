2Station two

What first-hand evidence is

First-hand evidence is something you know yourself. It comes in four kinds:

Personal experience

Something that happened to you. The most common kind.

Anecdotes

Stories you've heard from other people.

Observations

Things you've noticed by watching your school, team, town, or classmates.

General knowledge

What you know about events from following them.

Personal experience adds a human element. It appeals to pathos and makes an abstract issue feel real, which is why it works so well in introductions and conclusions.

4Station four

Speak as an insider

Personal experience works best when you can speak as an insider: someone with inside knowledge of the issue.

You can speak knowledgeably about single-sex classrooms because you know how classrooms work from the inside. A teacher, a principal, or a student at a single-sex school would each be an insider in a different way.

What it does well

  • Draws readers in
  • Makes an abstract issue human
  • Appeals to pathos
  • Builds credibility when you're an insider

Where it falls short

  • It's one person's view
  • Readers need more than your perspective to be persuaded
  • An outsider's experience carries less weight

Use personal experience to open the door. Then bring in other evidence, like facts, statistics, or expert views, to win the argument.
